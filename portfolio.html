COVID Response - PAPR Adapter

researchtriangle.org [1] | today.duke.edu [2] | vimeo.com [3] | sciencedirect.com [4] | ncbi.nlm.nih.gov [5]

At the beginning of the pandemic, PPE devices were significantly limited. Surgeons commonly use a system called the Stryker Flyte Suit that provides a sterile cover that protects patients during procedures. This product ecosystem is the most popular of its kind in the medical industry. It is designed to prevent biohazards from being transmitted from the physician to the patient and to stop fluid transfers from contaminants that originate with the patient. However, it does little to prevent aerosolized pathogens such as COVID from being transmitted to the doctor as the air intake of the suit is not filtered. With an emergency need readily apparent, a team was formed and new system components were designed to create filtration devices that would protect doctors working with COVID+ patients. As this was a breathing system, high standards of design and testing had to be achieved as any failure would, at best, expose the surgeon to pathogens and at worst, fail to provide them with enough air. Once the system was designed, it needed to be produced. A distributed manufacturing system was created that allowed for the production of hundreds of units. Though the system was patented, the products were distributed for free during the pandemic.

COVID Response - Face Shield

techtimes.com [1] | abc11.com [2] | today.duke.edu [3]

At the onset of COVID, healthcare providers were desperately seeking new ways to protect themselves during procedures. Face shields became an obvious tool to reduce viral transmission. These protective devices were uncommon for most procedures and screenings so the national inventory was low. Health systems scrambled to purchase PPE, warehouses were quickly depleted. As part of the Duke COVID Engineering Response Team, I took the project lead role to design and manufacture inventory to protect our providers.

Though a seemingly simple design, Face Shields are a Class II medical device and would face heavy scrutiny. From a design perspective, they would need to fit the faces of 30,000 providers, break free if caught but not fall off, be large enough to guarantee coverage but small enough to not interfere with devices such as stethoscopes, they need to survive sterilization, be chemically resistant, couldn’t shatter or have sharp edges, and they needed to fit within a small storage space. The most difficult requirement was manufacturing, we needed a device that could be manufactured on-site, using distributed manufacturing methods.

We would need to design, test and produce 100,000 shields in less than eight weeks. To accomplish this, I created a design and contributed to the open-source hardware community’s efforts to develop a concept that could meet our constraints. The first thousand prototypes were delivered using 3D printers while the assembly lines for mass manufacturing were established. Sourcing materials and equipment for manufacturing was nearly impossible. I got creative with atypical vendors to purchase raw materials. To form the plastic pizza ovens and shipping conveyor belts were purchased. A local sculpture studio was contracted for warehousing and labor support. Donors such as Hanes and Nike were engaged to ship elastic straps. PLC's were programmed for assembly lines. This project pushed every limit but ultimately was a massive success.

MediaSAN (UNC Wilmington Media Storage Area Network)

Storage Area Networks (SANs) are separate, out-of-band networks that power the high-density storage systems of most data centers. These networks allow for the transfer of data at speeds that significantly exceed what is possible with ethernet while providing dedicated pathways for data that is outside of the regular traffic pipelines. With the rise of the non-linear-video-editing in the early 2000’s many challenges with video storage were apparent. Editors did not have the capacity to edit collaboratively or store their projects on protected servers. Producers could not review projects without traveling to the editing suites. Master Control operators could not broadcast the digital files without first transferring them to tape media.

In this project, I created and developed a SAN that could provide storage capacity for our editing facility, master control/headend broadcast facility, and streaming media servers. This system provided a petabyte of storage, by 2010’s standards this was an unheard-of capacity. Twenty-two attached nodes were in place, given this network was not located only in a data center, and significant infrastructure had to be created. The deployment of this network created automation that saved countless hours of staff time. The interconnectivity of four facilities created massive labor savings that could be applied to production. This nearly doubled our output capacity. In this project, I served as the system architect, engineer, and once operational, the network administrator.

MediaNET (Duke University Storage Area Network)

The successful completion of the MediaSAN project and transition to operational service was noteworthy within the educational community. This project was a driving factor in my recruitment to Duke University which desired to implement such a program. The initial phase of the project involved selecting and deploying a large Isilon array, which while complicated in of itself was straightforward relative to the pure scale of Duke. The geography of Duke’s facilities is novel within higher education; the campus is a massive and sprawling 8,700 acres with satellite campuses located throughout the world. Unlike the deployment at UNC Wilmington where the campus was a single location spanning 600 acres, new technologies had to be leveraged that allowed us to realize the full potential of switched networks. The “push to the edge” concept was created to get ultra- high-speed networks closer to the “edge” where end users typically are. This project required significant stakeholder engagement, leadership and interdivisional work.
