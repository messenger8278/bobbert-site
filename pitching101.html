Part One / 01

What is a Pitch?

  • Pitches are not about money! They are about achieving a goal. PITCHES ARE JUST STORY TELLING
    • Given this truth, pitches are a tool entrepreneurs can use in a variety of situations from marketing to events to funding to leadership purposes.
  • Game Theory Applies
    • What is your objective? You are winning something with this talk, what is it? Be clear on what you are trying to achieve, "getting the investment" may not actually be winning.
  • Why do pitches fail?
    • Most pitches fail not because the idea is bad, but because it’s told without context.
    • A great pitch doesn’t just tell your story; it connects that story to your audience’s goals.
    • Investors, foundations, and partners each have portfolios, collections of ventures that together serve a strategic purpose. If your pitch doesn’t help them achieve their purpose, they won’t help you achieve yours.
Part One / 02

You Are a Storyteller: The Theater Model of the Pitch

A pitch is like a three-act play. It must have tension, movement, and resolution.

Act I - The Setup (Problem + Context)

  • Introduce the world: Who’s affected? What’s broken?
  • Show the conflict: The problem creates emotional tension.
  • Reveal the insight: Your “aha” moment, what you discovered that others missed.

💡 Traditional match: Problem → Opportunity → Market

Act II - The Confrontation (Solution + Validation)

  • Present your solution: The protagonist (you) responds to the conflict (dissatisfaction).
  • Show your evidence: Prototype, traction, or customer feedback.
  • Introduce your allies: The team, mentors, or partners who help you overcome obstacles.

💡 Traditional match: Solution → Product → Traction → Competition

Act III - The Resolution (Vision + Ask)

  • Paint the future: What does the world look like if you succeed? What if you don't?
  • Explain sustainability: Business model, growth path, or social impact.
  • Deliver the ask: What do you need and why now?

💡 Traditional match: Business Model → Market Growth → Ask / Vision

Impact InvestorMeasurable social/environmental outcomesShow evidence of impact and a path to sustainability.

🎯 Ask yourself:

“How does my success advance their portfolio’s success?”

That question is the difference between being interesting and being investable.

Part One / 04

The One-Pager

A one-pager is the calling card of your venture, a short, visual summary that accompanies your pitch. It lives on after your presentation. It’s how mentors, judges, or investors remember you once you’ve left the room.

🎯 Purpose

Think of the one pager as a copy of a resume that you would bring to a job interview.

It’s a professional summary, not a flyer. Think: What would someone need to decide if we’re worth a second meeting?

🕐 When to Give It

TimingDeliveryWhy It Matters
Before the Pitch (Pre-read)Email/upload 24–48 hrs aheadGives reviewers time to prepare questions.
At the PitchPrint clean copies (no staples)Provides a tangible reference for judges.
After the PitchSend digital copy within 24 hrsKeeps your idea top-of-mind during decisions.

✍️ Design Basics

  • One page only.
  • Readable in 60 seconds.
  • Logo, sketch, or photos are ok, but keep them small.
  • Use whitespace; avoid dense paragraphs.

🧩 Content Guide (Use the 3-Act Framework)

Don't actually use the words "Act I, II, III", but follow this content pattern of problem, solution, vision.

SectionContentQuick Tip
Act I – ProblemWho is affected and why it mattersOpen with a one-sentence hook.
Act II – SolutionWhat you built and how it worksInclude a visual or diagram.
Act III – VisionWhy now and what you needBe specific about your ask.
FooterTeam names + contact infoDouble-check accuracy.

💬 How to Hand It Over

  • Supply it as a pre-read if possible, email it in advance to your relationship manager.
  • Hand it out intentionally and confidently.
  • Say one sentence: “This is our one-page summary of our problem, solution, and traction.”
  • Never read from it, it’s for them, not you.
  • Email follow-up within a day that offers to supply additional information for their analysts. Your goal is to make the analysts' job easy with your follow up communication.

🚫 Avoid

  • Walls of text or tiny fonts.
  • Unclear ask (“We just want feedback”).
  • Missing contact info.
  • Over-designed marketing flyers.

🧠 Remember:

The pitch earns attention.
The one-pager earns investment.

Part One / 05

Analysts & Associates Rule

The investor on the Zoom call may nod politely, but the analyst team writes the memo that lands on the partner’s desk. They’re the ones who:

  • Are the 'top of the funnel' filter mechanism
  • Whose job is to verify claims and numbers.
  • Compare your deal to the firm’s existing portfolio.
  • Flag red and green signals for fit, scalability, and credibility.

Tips:

  • A strong pre-read makes their job easy, and that earns internal advocates.
  • Be Accurate - Analysts will eviscerate any pitch or document with questionable or unsupportable claims.
  • Make sure your logic is clear. Don't just present numbers, numbers should be a part of your story and presented in a way that is easy to follow. Make it clear why they are relevant to your project and where the numbers are sourced.
Part One / 06

Key Takeaways

  • Emotion before data. Make them care, then make them calculate.
  • Conflict drives attention. Problems create narrative tension.
  • Fit matters. Know their thesis, stage, geography, and values.
  • The ask is earned. It should feel like the story’s natural ending.
  • Analysts and Associates. They will be your strongest advocates or worst enemy.

In short:

A powerful pitch tells a compelling story that fits perfectly inside the audience’s existing goals. Don’t just tell them what you do, tell them why it belongs in their world.

Part Two / Partnership

The pitch isn’t just about you. This is a partnership.

Part Two / 01

The Pitch Is a Two-Way Interview

Investors, foundations, and partners aren’t just buying into your idea; they’re becoming part of your story. When you pitch, you’re not only selling, you’re testing for alignment in values, expectations, and working style.

Ask yourself during or after every pitch:

  • Do they understand our mission?
  • Are their questions tactical or strategic?
  • Do they care about short-term returns or long-term impact?
  • Would I want to work with them when things get hard?
  • Look for 'predatory lender' language within their questions but do not address it directly.

The way they engage tells you what kind of partner they’ll be.

Part Two / 02

Observation = Data

Every reaction, note, and question during a pitch gives you market intelligence:

  • Which slides or claims made them lean forward? (That’s what resonates.)
  • Where did they hesitate or press for data? (That’s where you lack clarity.)
  • What follow-up questions did they send afterward? (That’s what they value.)

You’re not just performing, you’re running an experiment in real time.

Part Two / 03

Use It to Shape Your Strategy

By pitching to multiple audiences, you collect feedback loops that refine your venture:

Feedback TypeWhat It RevealsHow to Use It
Analyst QuestionsWhat’s measurableImprove your data storytelling.
Partner CuriosityWhat’s strategically usefulReframe your value proposition.
Investor HesitationWhat’s risky or unclearPatch credibility gaps before the next pitch.

You’re learning how the market thinks about your idea. That’s powerful intelligence.

Part Two / 04

Smart Founders Interview Back

A good entrepreneur comes prepared with a few gentle reverse questions — especially in later-stage discussions:

  • "Do you see an opportunity for value beyond dollars with this relationship?"
  • “How hands-on are you after investment?”
  • “What’s your follow-on strategy?”
  • “What does success look like for your firm beyond ROI?”

You’re showing maturity, that you see this as a partnership, not just a transaction.

Part Two / 05

The Mindset Shift

Most people pitch to impress.
Great founders pitch to learn.

Every “no” teaches you how to get to “yes.” Every skeptical question reveals how professionals view risk. Every meeting gives you a clearer picture of which investors are right for you.
